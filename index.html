Stochastic Penetration Process: n Systems & m Attackers

Asymptotic behavior of Total Score Sn, Average Score Sn/n (Law of Large Numbers), and Normalized Score Sn/√n (Central Limit Theorem) under Bernoulli and Rademacher increments

1. Stochastic Models & Scaling Laws Bernoulli X_i ∈ {0, 1} vs. Rademacher X_i ∈ {−1, +1}

We model m independent attackers attempting to penetrate n sequential systems. For each attacker, the step-i increment Xi represents the score on system i, with X1, …, Xn independent and identically distributed (mean μ = E[Xi], variance σ2 = Var(Xi)). Two increment models are studied:

  • Bernoulli Model: Xi ∈ {0, 1} with P(Xi = 1) = p and P(Xi = 0) = 1 − p. Here μ = p and σ2 = p(1 − p). Sn counts the total number of compromised systems.
  • Rademacher Model: Xi ∈ {−1, +1} with P(Xi = +1) = P(Xi = −1) = 1/2. This symmetric zero-mean walk has μ = 0 and σ2 = 1.

5. Theoretical Analysis & Cybersecurity Interpretation LLN, Variance Normalizations, CLT & System-Attacker Dynamics

1. Convergence in Probability of Sn/n (Weak Law of Large Numbers): Because Var(Sn/n) = σ2/n → 0 as n → ∞, Chebyshev's inequality guarantees that for every tolerance ε > 0:

P( |S_n / n - μ| > ε ) ≤ Var(S_n / n) / ε^2 = σ^2 / (n ε^2) → 0 as n → ∞

Thus Sn/n →P μ (p in the Bernoulli model, 0 in the Rademacher model). As seen in Section 3 and 4, the histogram of Sn/n collapses into a Dirac-like spike around μ and the empirical tail probability P(|Sn/n − μ| > 0.05) rapidly drops to zero (exponentially fast by Hoeffding/Chernoff bounds, well below the conservative 1/n Chebyshev upper bound).

2. Behavior of the Variance under the Three Normalizations: Scaling Sn by n−α multiplies its variance by n−2α, yielding Var(n−αSn) = n1−2ασ2. On the log–log plot in Section 4:

  • For Sn (α = 0), Var(Sn) = nσ2 grows linearly (log–log slope +1): the trajectories fan out indefinitely.
  • For Sn/n (α = 1), Var(Sn/n) = σ2/n decays hyperbolically (log–log slope −1): random fluctuations are over-damped and vanish.
  • For Sn/√n (α = 1/2), the exponent 1 − 2α = 0 cancels n exactly, leaving a constant variance Var(Sn/√n) = σ2 for all n (log–log slope 0).

3. Convergence in Distribution of Sn/√n (Central Limit Theorem): By the classical Lindeberg–Lévy Central Limit Theorem, the standardized sum (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) converges in distribution to the standard normal N(0, 1). In the Rademacher model, μ = 0 and σ = 1, so the uncentered normalized score Sn/√n is already standardized and converges directly:

S_n / √n →^d N(0, 1) (Rademacher case, μ = 0, σ^2 = 1)

This is confirmed both visually (the histogram of Sn/√n transitions from two discrete spikes at ±1 for n = 1 into the bell curve φ(z)) and quantitatively via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic D_n = sup_z |F_m(z) - Φ(z)|, which decays toward the Monte Carlo noise floor O(1/√m). In contrast, for the Bernoulli model (μ = p > 0), E[Sn/√n] = p√n → +∞, so the distribution of Sn/√n retains constant variance p(1 − p) while drifting rightward at rate √n; subtracting p√n and dividing by √(p(1 − p)) recovers N(0, 1).

4. Interpretation for n Systems and m Attackers:

  • Role of System Count (n): As an organization scales its infrastructure to n systems, the expected number of breached systems per attacker grows linearly as np, while the absolute uncertainty grows slower, as √n. However, each attacker's fractional compromise rate Sn/n locks deterministically onto their skill parameter p (LLN). Merely adding more systems does not dilute the fraction of compromised hosts—only hardening defenses (lowering p) reduces the breach rate. In the symmetric Rademacher defense/offense game (μ = 0), even though the average advantage per system is zero, random fluctuations of order √n mean some attackers still achieve large positive net penetration scores purely by chance.
  • Role of Attacker Count (m): Increasing m does not alter the stochastic law of any individual attacker's trajectory, but it governs aggregate and extreme-value risk. Across all m attackers, the total breaches sum to nm i.i.d. trials with mean nmp and variance nmp(1 − p). Moreover, from the defender's perspective, a system is compromised if at least one of the m independent attackers succeeds, raising the per-system compromise probability from p to 1 - (1 - p)^m (which rapidly approaches 1 as m grows), while the maximum score among m attackers shifts into the upper tail of the normal distribution (≈ np + σ√(n · 2 ln m)).
